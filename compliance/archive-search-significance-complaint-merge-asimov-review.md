## What This Automation Does

Two AI calls run per Missive conversation, against an archive of 254,000+ real tenant/owner/vendor email threads:

- **Call 1** (every eligible conversation): tags topic (8-value taxonomy), resolution status, a Fair Housing protected-class self-check, tone trend, and identity (via deterministic address-match, with a cite-then-lookup fallback).
- **Call 2** (conditional — most non-routine/unresolved conversations): assigns an urgency signal (5 values, folded in from the never-run complaint-tracking tool), an honest "needs a human call" flag, and — for owner instructions — drafts note text, including a specific present-day-assessment template for instructions found in old mail.

Results write to two new tables (`missive_conversation_significance`, `complaints`) and surface on Property 360, a dashboard "Needs Attention"/"Historical Backlog" split, and (for live mail only) a home-page red-badge tile with DO assignment and a 24-hour aging nudge. Nothing in this design sends a message to any tenant, owner, or vendor — confirmed, this stays an internal triage/browsing tool. No housing decision (approve/deny/condition) is made anywhere in this design.

## Tier Classification

| Action | Tier | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Call 1 tagging, writes to `missive_conversation_significance` | **Tier 1** | Internal record-creation only, no communication, no decision. Matches GOVERNANCE.md's Tier 1 definition. |
| Call 2 urgency signal, `needs_human_call`, draft `note_text` | **Tier 1** for the write itself (a draft, held for human review via `proposeAINote()`'s existing approval gate) | Nothing is sent or acted on by the AI. |
| Home-page red-badge tile, DO assignment (`owner_team_member_id`), 24-hour aging nudge — live mail only | **Tier 2** | Surfaces to a human for action; human decides what to do. Not Tier 3 because no decision is being made *for* anyone yet — but see Finding 5 below on whether this machinery actually fires reliably. |
| A historical `owner_instruction_rejected = true` finding (a possible discriminatory instruction discovered in old mail) | **Should be Tier 3** | This is Fair-Housing-adjacent evidence of a possible historical violation with an unknown resolution. It must escalate entirely, not sit in a passive browsing tab — see Finding 2 below. |

## Findings

### 1. The privilege-filter question (spec Section 7 item 7, Section 11 item 5) — real, and worse than the spec's own framing suggests. This needs a fresh attorney opinion, not an Asimov judgment call.

I read `checkThread()` (`email-intake/lib/privilege-filter.js`) directly. It is not a Fair-Housing check — it is a four-layer, purpose-built attorney-client-privilege/legal-matter filter: law-firm domain detection, an attorney/litigation/regulator keyword scan, a staff manual override, and a thread-level default-deny (one message trips it, the whole thread holds). This is a different instrument catching a different thing than Fair Housing screening, and the spec correctly says so.

Two facts make this more than a hypothetical "different things, worth asking":

- **`subject-match.js`'s own header states, in so many words, why complaint-tracking's original design called `checkThread()` before anything else:** "the original draft ran an address-to-identity match on EVERY thread, BEFORE the hold check — associating raw, unscreened correspondence with a real tenant/owner/vendor row before `privilege-filter.js`'s `checkThread()` had a chance to pull a privileged thread out of automated processing." Both Asimov and Mason independently confirmed this ordering fix on 2026-09-10 as closing a real gap (`complaint-tracking-technical-spec.md` lines 32, 99-101, 288, 290). **The merged design removes `checkThread()` from this pipeline entirely.** `subject-match.js`'s own file header — unedited by this merge — now describes a precondition ("this function is never called until AFTER `checkThread()` has already run, on either branch, no exception") that is simply false under the new design, because there is no `checkThread()` call anywhere in the merged pass. That is not a stale comment to clean up later; it is the exact safety property two prior governance reviews treated as load-bearing, now silently absent.
- **The population most likely to contain privileged material is not hypothetical — it is a specific, countable set of real rows.** Archive-search's own screening pass called this same `checkThread()` (confirmed in `compliance/archive-search-held-release-asimov-confirmation.md`: "in `screening-pass.js`, `runScreeningPassChunk()` calls `checkThread()` first"). Before the held-release change, 3,623 conversations tripped it — law-firm domains, litigation/attorney keywords, or a staff legal-hold tag. That change (approved on a **search-visibility** theory: "Archive Search should not be Rincon's privilege-determination engine," per outside counsel) reprocessed those 3,623 through Fair Housing screening only and returned them to the searchable pool. **This merge's Call 2 is specifically designed to read for `legal_exposure`, decide urgency, and draft permanent `note_text` — over exactly that reinstated population, with zero privilege screening anywhere in the pipeline.** Outside counsel's opinion was never asked this question. It was asked, and answered, "does making already-accessible mail *searchable* require a privilege filter" — with an explicit factual predicate ("Rincon's actual attorney communications... are ordinarily handled through separate named company email accounts") that reduced the real-world risk of privileged material actually sitting in the indexed archive. Counsel was never asked whether an AI *actively characterizing legal exposure and generating new written work product* about that same content — work product that becomes a new, permanent, queryable business record admin/DO can read — needs its own screen. Those are different acts: one is retrieval, the other is synthesis and record-creation.

**My judgment:** this is a real, unresolved gap, not adequately closed by the existing search-visibility opinion, and I am not resolving it myself — it is Mason's and outside counsel's to answer, per my own role limits. **This is a condition, not a note for later:** a fresh, specifically-scoped attorney question is required before Q builds Call 2, framed around (a) AI-drafted characterizations of `legal_exposure` becoming permanent business records, and (b) the specific, non-hypothetical population of formerly-legal-hold-flagged conversations now fully exposed to that AI with no privilege filter in the new pipeline at all.

### 2. The retroactive `owner_instruction_rejected` wording (Section 3, Section 5) — the fix is real but incomplete, and the document's own carve-out (Section 6, Item 12) leaves exactly this category out of mandatory human review. I'm resolving that second half now.

The wording change (never claiming what Rincon "said," only what an AI today assesses an old instruction "would require") correctly avoids fabricating a historical fact. I agree it is a genuine improvement over v1 and agree it needs its own attorney sign-off distinct from the live-script approval, exactly as Section 11 Item 6 says.

But the wording fix does not, by itself, resolve the underlying risk — it relocates it. What gets created is a new, dated, permanent record stating that Rincon's own AI identified what reads as a historical Fair Housing violation, paired with an explicit admission that nobody knows what was actually done about it. That is a materially different, and arguably higher-stakes, artifact than the thing it replaces (silence). Whether that specific record should exist at all, and in what form, is exactly the kind of question that needs a lawyer who has been told what the record will actually say and where it will actually live — not inherited from an opinion written for a live-response script.

Separately — and this is my own finding, not just an endorsement of Section 11 Item 6 — **Section 6's mandatory-clearance checklist, as specced, does not include this exact case.** The checklist criteria (Section 6, "The one carve-out") are `category IN ('legal_exposure', 'major_money_property_risk') OR escalation_signal = 'blocked_resolution'`, AND unresolved. `owner_instruction` is not in that list. The document's own Section 11 Item 12 flags this gap honestly and leaves it open. I am closing it: **a historical `owner_instruction_rejected = TRUE` row must be added to the mandatory-clearance criteria in Section 6.** This is the single most Fair-Housing-sensitive output this whole design produces — a documented instance of what looks like discriminatory conduct — and under the Fair Housing Standard's Rule 7 (human-in-the-loop) and GOVERNANCE.md Rule 8 (Tier 3 items escalate entirely, never just get tagged for optional browsing), it cannot be left to sit on a passive "Historical Backlog" tab that nobody is required to open. I am not extending this requirement to `churn_risk`/`escalation_recurrence`/bare `needs_human_call` (Section 11 Item 12's other open question) — those are business-risk signals, not Fair-Housing-adjacent legal findings, and leaving them as tab-only browsing is a reasonable product call, not a governance gap.

### 3. The live/historical firewall (Section 6) — the query-level gating is sound; the "must never be silently suppressed" half is not actually load-bearing yet, for both historical *and* live mail. I'm requiring a fix to Section 4's `complaints`-creation trigger, not just the historical carve-out.

The hard-gate mechanism (aging job, red-badge tile, and DO assignment scoped to `discovery_context = 'live_pipeline'` at the query level) correctly closes the "pages the DO about a 2019 dispute" failure mode — that part is real, structural, and I have no objection to it.

The "must never be silently suppressed" half is weaker than the document's own language claims, for a reason that goes beyond what Section 11 Item 12 already flags:

- **The mandatory-clearance checklist is a view someone has to think to open.** Nothing in Section 6 describes an active push — a one-time notification when the historical backfill finishes and `complaints_historical_review_required` is non-empty, or any other mechanism that puts this in front of Peter/the DO without them first deciding to go look. "A discrete list... must affirmatively clear" describes an obligation once someone opens the tab; it does not describe how anyone is told to open it. As specced, this is functionally the same "tagged for optional browsing" pattern GOVERNANCE.md Rule 8 exists to prevent — even though the document's own governance-lens language (quoted in Section 6) already named the failure mode correctly. **Required before build:** an explicit, active notification (in whatever channel Peter already uses for this — the same "outside the Hub" pattern the held-release review used for privilege escalation) fired once per backfill run, naming the count and linking directly to the unclear checklist.
- **Section 11 Item 13, which the document itself raises and leaves open, is a live-mail problem, not just a historical one.** A conversation can be `is_big_issue = TRUE` purely on `category IN ('legal_exposure', 'owner_instruction')` (Section 6's generated column), with `escalation_signal = 'none'` and `needs_human_call = FALSE`. Per Section 4, a `complaints` row — the only thing that drives the red-badge tile, DO assignment, and 24-hour aging nudge — is created only when `escalation_signal != 'none'`, or `needs_human_call`, or `owner_instruction_rejected`. **A `legal_exposure`-category live email that doesn't happen to also trip one of those three conditions therefore never reaches the DO at all** — no badge, no assignment, no aging clock — even though it is, by the document's own definition, a big issue. It only ever shows up on Property 360 or a "Needs Attention" tab someone has to browse to. This is the exact live-mail equivalent of the historical-suppression failure mode Section 6 was written to prevent, and it is not an edge case — it is the design's literal, stated behavior for a real and expected category of email (e.g. an ordinary habitability/code-enforcement mention that never separately reads as "blocked" or triggers uncertainty). **Required before build:** Section 4's `complaints`-creation trigger must also fire when `category IN ('legal_exposure', 'owner_instruction')`, so every `is_big_issue = TRUE` live conversation reliably reaches the paging machinery, not only the subset that happens to also carry an escalation signal.

With both of those closed, I agree the mechanism satisfies Rule 8 for both directions (no false alarms on stale mail, no silent suppression of real ones). As specced today, it satisfies only the first half.

### 4. Rule 6's three prongs — status, checked against what Peter has actually said, not the document's framing of itself

- **Owner approval:** Peter has given a scope directive ("fold the six categories into one AI read, don't build a fourth pass") and one explicit product decision (`is_big_issue`'s corrected definition, Section 6/9/11 Item 1; the strict Property 360 scope, Section 9). Neither of those is an approval of the compliance risk of running this at 254,000-conversation bulk scale. **Not given yet**, and Section 12 says so itself ("each is Peter's to request next, separately").
- **Attorney review:** the three existing opinions on record (held-release, layer1-removal, self-report-recalibration) all answer questions about search *visibility* and Fair Housing *screening*. None was asked, or answers, the two new questions this merge raises: an AI drafting permanent legal-exposure characterizations (Finding 1), or a present-day record characterizing a possible historical Fair Housing violation (Finding 2). **Not given.**
- **Shadow mode:** the existing 14-day, "review 100% of output" plan is explicitly obsolete at this scale (Section 12 says so). No replacement stratified-sample exit criterion has been chosen by Peter. **Not decided.**

All three are open. I confirm the document's own conclusion here without qualification.

### 5. Retiring complaint-tracking's own pipeline (Section 8) — the right call, with one thing to correct in how it's described

I agree a single merged pass is safer than two independent AI categorizers silently disagreeing over the same mail, and I confirm nothing in the original 2026-09-10 approval (`complaint-tracking-ai-risk-assessment.md`, `complaint-tracking-technical-spec.md`) ever framed running two independent categorizers as a deliberate safety feature — the original design was a single categorizer preceded by a single privilege gate; the "redundancy" this merge removes was never a designed-in safety property, it was an accident of two tools independently wanting to read the same mail. Retiring the dormant tool in favor of one source of truth is the right governance call.

The one thing worth being precise about, since it connects directly to Finding 1: retiring `process-pending-messages.js` does not just retire "an AI-categorization call and its own driver query," as Section 8 puts it. It also retires the *only* privilege gate that ever stood in front of that AI call. Framing it as a clean, low-risk retirement understates what's actually being given up. That doesn't change my agreement with the decision — it changes what has to be true before the decision is safe to execute, which is Finding 1's condition.

## Verdict

**NOT CLEARED.**

The design work in this document is genuinely careful, and I agree with the large majority of its judgment calls (the driver-query fix, the two-call split, the fail-closed-by-field posture, retiring complaint-tracking's pipeline, the `is_big_issue` correction's mechanics). But real, specific gates are still open, and Peter asked explicitly for a rigorous first-pass, not a fast one. What's missing before Neo applies a migration or Q writes code:

1. **A fresh, specifically-scoped attorney question** on whether an AI pass that reads for `legal_exposure` and drafts permanent notes — over a source pool that includes the 3,623 conversations that previously tripped the attorney-privilege/legal-hold filter before that filter was removed for search purposes — needs a privilege pre-filter of its own. The existing held-release opinion answered a different question (search visibility) and does not cover this. (Finding 1)
2. **A fresh attorney sign-off, specific to the retroactive `owner_instruction_rejected` template**, distinct from the live-script approval — on record, on the exact wording that will actually ship. (Finding 2; Section 11 Item 6)
3. **Section 6's mandatory-clearance checklist extended to include historical `owner_instruction_rejected = TRUE` rows.** (Finding 2 — resolving Section 11 Item 12 for this one case.)
4. **An active notification when the historical backfill's mandatory-clearance list is non-empty** — not just a view someone has to think to open. (Finding 3)
5. **Section 4's `complaints`-creation trigger extended to fire on `category IN ('legal_exposure', 'owner_instruction')` alone**, so every `is_big_issue` live conversation reliably reaches the DO/badge/aging machinery, not only the subset that also trips an escalation signal. (Finding 3 — resolving Section 11 Item 13.)
6. **A standalone, fresh AI Risk Assessment document** for this specific build (bulk historical scale, retroactive record generation, the reinstated legal-hold population, the merged trigger surface) — the existing one was explicitly scoped to a daily trickle and says so itself.
7. **Peter's own, explicit, on-the-record approval of this specific compliance risk** — distinct from the scope directive and the `is_big_issue`/Property-360 product decisions already given — including a bulk-appropriate shadow-mode exit criterion (a sized, stratified sample, not "review all of it").
8. **A completed Rule 4 data inventory for the merged schema** (both tables, as they'll actually ship — `discovery_context`, `historical_review_cleared_*`, the retroactive template — not the original 2026-09-10 inventory).
9. **Mason's own review**, on the eight-category/five-signal vocabulary and everything named above that is inside Fair Housing's domain rather than mine.

Once 1-5 are resolved and 6-9 are on record, I'd expect this to clear — the underlying design is sound and the team has already found and fixed most of its own real problems. It isn't there yet.
