# Self-Managing vs. Hiring a Property Manager: A Real Cost Comparison

Most landlords who self-manage do it for one reason: the management fee looks like pure savings. Skip the 8-12% and keep it, right? That instinct makes sense on paper — but it only holds up if the monthly fee is the only cost in the comparison. It isn't. Self-managing has real costs too; they just don't show up as a line item, so they're easy to miss until they've already cost you money.

Here's the honest, side-by-side math.

## What a Property Manager Actually Costs

The core monthly fee for a single-family rental typically runs **8-12% of collected rent**, averaging close to 8.5% nationally. But that's rarely the whole bill. Most management agreements also include a leasing or placement fee (often 50-100% of one month's rent when a new tenant is placed), plus renewal and inspection fees. Add those up and the **all-in first-year cost typically lands between 14-20% of gross rent** — front-loaded in years with a tenant turnover, lighter in years where a good tenant just stays.

That's a real cost, and it should be spelled out plainly, not buried. But it's also a *known, capped* cost. You can see it coming. The self-managing side of the ledger is where the surprises live.

## What Self-Managing Actually Costs (Even Though You Won't Get a Bill For It)

**Vacancy is the big one.** A property that sits vacant longer costs you every single day it's empty — and self-managed listings often take longer to fill, since they typically reach fewer channels and get priced on gut feel rather than current market data. The math is blunt: a unit that sits empty an **extra 24 days** (45 days vacant instead of 21) can cost **over $1,400 in lost rent** on a modest rental — and that number scales up fast on a higher-rent property. One slow re-lease can wipe out most of a year's "savings" in a single stretch.

**Your time isn't free.** Studies on self-managing landlords put the average time commitment at **4-8 hours a month** for a single rental — tenant communication, maintenance coordination, rent follow-up, recordkeeping — and that's the *quiet* baseline. During a turnover or a difficult tenant situation, it climbs well past that. If your time is worth anything close to what you earn elsewhere, this is a real cost, even though you'll never see it on a bank statement.

**You pay retail for maintenance.** A management company sending steady volume to its vendors negotiates rates a one-property landlord can't get. Each individual repair might only cost a little more at retail — but across a year of ordinary wear and tear, it adds up, and you're also the one spending an evening finding and scheduling the vendor.

**Eviction risk is the expensive tail case.** Most tenancies go fine. But when one doesn't, it's expensive — a single eviction, once you count lost rent, legal costs, and turnover repairs, can run **$4,000-$7,000 or more** in total impact. It doesn't have to happen often to matter; it just has to happen once.

**Add it up, and the pattern is consistent:** once vacancy loss, missed rent optimization, retail maintenance pricing, and the value of your own time are counted honestly, self-management commonly runs **$2,000-$5,000 more per year** than professional management would have cost — not less.

## So When Does Self-Managing Actually Win?

It genuinely does, in the right situation. Self-managing tends to make sense when you're local and responsive, your property is newer and low-maintenance, your tenant has stayed long-term, and your time isn't stretched thin by a job or other properties. Fast response times shrink vacancy. Good recordkeeping shrinks legal risk. If that's your situation, the fee savings can be real.

It tends to *not* pencil out when the property turns over often, you're managing from a distance, you're time-constrained, or you're newer to the legal and compliance side — because that's exactly where the expensive surprises tend to live.
